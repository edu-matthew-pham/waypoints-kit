I assumed a Steady class, one lesson per waypoint except the two hinge waypoints (2 and 3) and the construction waypoint (4), which get two each. A waypoint is a step in the fixed sequence every student passes through; a hinge waypoint is one where a weak understanding causes problems later.

## Waypoint 1: Purpose of classification  [width opportunity]

Students start knowing familiar animals and plants and can sort them into broad groups. After this waypoint they can say why scientists classify living things, which gives the later work on kingdoms and keys a purpose. Waypoint 2 needs students to see classification as a tool for identifying and communicating, not just putting things in piles.

:::keep-together

### Lesson 1.1: Why bother classifying?
**Focus:** Students compare a useful classification (such as a supermarket or library layout) with a plain alphabetical list, then give two reasons scientists classify living things.

**Shape:** orient → anchor → build → check

**Cliff notes:**
- **Likely trouble spot:** Students say classification is "putting things in groups" or "sorting by colour", which describes the action but not the reason.
- **What to say:** Ask "What can you do with a classified list that you can't do with an alphabetical one?" Steer towards identifying an unknown thing and sharing a name everyone understands. Don't name kingdoms yet.
- **If it isn't landing:** Give two different sorts of the same ten animals (by habitat, by body features) and ask which one would help a stranger work out what an unknown animal is.
- **Watch-out:** The wider ideas (First Nations classification, microscopy, biosecurity) enrich this lesson only; don't use them to teach keys or reclassification early.

{{columns 22% 26% 26% 26%}}
| Minimum width | Consolidating | Steady | Advancing |
|---|---|---|---|
| States at least two reasons scientists classify living things | Choose from a sentence starter bank: "so we can ... " | Write own two reasons after the supermarket versus alphabetical comparison | Compare Linnaean and a First Nations system on Country: what does each prioritise? |
| Gives one correct everyday example of classification | Pick from a set of photos (library, pharmacy, wardrobe) and say what is being sorted | Offer an example from their own life and say what it helps you do | Explain how classification supports identification in biosecurity or medicine |

## Waypoint 2: Taxonomy: major groups and kingdoms  [hinge]

This builds on the idea that classification has a purpose. After it, students can place familiar organisms into kingdoms using what they can observe and justify the choice. Waypoint 3 needs that skill: if students can't judge which features separate groups, they can't follow or later construct a key. Cascade risk: students who can't reliably assign organisms to kingdoms can't choose discriminating features when building keys at waypoint 4, so this waypoint gets two lessons and a varied set of organisms.

:::keep-together

### Lesson 2.1: Sorting by what you can see
**Focus:** Students name the kingdoms and place five familiar organisms (including a mushroom, a whale and a bacterium image) into the correct kingdom, justifying each with a feature they can observe.

**Shape:** review → teach → practise → check

**Cliff notes:**
- **Likely trouble spot:** Students sort by habitat or use rather than features, for example "a mushroom is a plant because it grows in soil" or "a whale is a fish because it lives in the sea".
- **What to say:** Ask "What can you see that tells you?" rather than "What is it?". Push for features such as how it gets food, whether it has leaves or spores, and how it moves.
- **If it isn't landing:** Give a one-line feature card per kingdom and have students match each organism to the card before naming the kingdom.
- **Watch-out:** Confirm which kingdom list your school uses before the lesson. [teacher: confirm five or six kingdoms]

| Minimum width | Consolidating | Steady | Advancing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Names the major kingdoms | Use a labelled kingdom chart as a reference during the task | Recall the kingdoms after a short teaching and recall activity | Use the Linnaean levels from kingdom down to species and say what each level represents |
| Places 5 familiar organisms into kingdoms with a justification based on observable features | Match organisms to feature cards, then write one justification sentence | Sort the five organisms and write a feature-based justification for each | Compare features within and between groups using specimens or images |

:::

:::keep-together

### Lesson 2.2: When the sorting gets hard
**Focus:** Students sort a mixed set of 12 organisms into kingdoms, then pick one that was hard to place (such as Euglena) and explain what made it difficult.

**Shape:** practise → confront → resolve → check

**Cliff notes:**
- **Likely trouble spot:** Students think a hard-to-place organism means they've made a mistake, rather than seeing that the difficulty is real information about the organism.
- **What to say:** Name the difficulty as a feature of the organism: "Euglena photosynthesises like a plant but moves like an animal. What does that tell us about the groups?"
- **If it isn't landing:** Work one boundary case through together on the board, listing features that fit each kingdom, before students pick their own.
- **Watch-out:** Include organisms from every kingdom, including fungi and protists, so students can't rely on animal and plant guesses. Keep keys out of this lesson; that is waypoint 3.

| Minimum width | Consolidating | Steady | Advancing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Correctly places familiar organisms into kingdoms with feature justification | Sort 12 organisms with feature cards still available | Sort 12 organisms using their own feature notes | Sort 12 organisms without cards and justify the harder placements |
| Identifies at least one organism that is difficult to classify and explains why | Choose from a short list of tricky organisms and complete a "fits ... but also ..." frame | Pick one from the set and explain what made it hard | Research a boundary case (Euglena or a virus): does it fit the kingdom model? |

## Waypoint 3: Using dichotomous keys  [hinge]

This builds on students' ability to describe organisms by observable features. After it, students can use a key to identify organisms and say which feature each decision point checks. Waypoint 4 asks students to reverse the process and build a key, which only works if they understand why each yes/no question splits the group. Cascade risk: students who follow a key by pattern-matching, without the binary logic, build keys that are structurally broken, so this waypoint gets two lessons and visible reasoning at every step.

:::keep-together

### Lesson 3.1: Following a key, saying why
**Focus:** Students use one provided key to identify five organisms, stating aloud at each decision point what feature is being checked and what the two outcomes are.

**Shape:** demonstrate → practise → check

**Cliff notes:**
- **Likely trouble spot:** Students follow the arrows without reading the feature, or choose whichever of the two options "sounds closer" instead of testing the organism against both.
- **What to say:** Model the question before each decision point: "What am I checking, and what are the two possible answers?" Have students point to the feature on the organism before they choose.
- **If it isn't landing:** Run the first two organisms as a guided think-aloud with the key on the board, then have pairs take turns playing "the key" and "the identifier".
- **Watch-out:** Don't ask students to write a key yet. Vague terms such as "large" in a key are worth pausing on, but only as a point about reading the decision.

{{columns 22% 26% 26% 26%}}
| Minimum width | Consolidating | Steady | Advancing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Follows all decision points in a provided key to correct identifications | Use a key with a feature annotated beside each decision point | Use the plain key and identify five organisms | Identify organisms surveyed in the local environment using a provided key |
| Names the observable feature used at each decision point | Complete a "I am checking ... and the answers are ... or ..." frame | Name the feature at each decision point in their own words | Spot a decision point whose wording is ambiguous and say how it could mislead |

:::

:::keep-together

### Lesson 3.2: Two keys, one set, and a faulty key
**Focus:** Students use two different keys on the same set of organisms, note where they agree and disagree, then diagnose a key that has one deliberate error.

**Shape:** practise → confront → resolve → check

**Cliff notes:**
- **Likely trouble spot:** When a key gives a wrong result, students assume they misjudged the organism rather than considering that the key's question might be flawed.
- **What to say:** Ask "Is the problem the organism or the question?" and have students trace the path that led to the wrong answer.
- **If it isn't landing:** Give a faulty key with the error already flagged by a coloured arrow, and ask only for the consequence and a proposed fix.
- **Watch-out:** Keep the correction to the faulty key to one decision point. Rewriting a whole key edges into waypoint 4.

{{columns 22% 26% 26% 26%}}
| Minimum width | Consolidating | Steady | Advancing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Follows all decision points in a provided key to correct identifications | Re-use the annotated key from 3.1 on a new organism set | Identify a new organism set with two plain keys | Compare a scientific key with a First Nations classification of the same organisms: what does each prioritise? |
| Explains why an incorrect path leads to a wrong outcome | Trace the wrong path with a highlighter and complete a "this went wrong because ..." frame | Find where the wrong path split and explain the consequence | Evaluate the flawed key, propose a correction, and explain the effect of the error |

## Waypoint 4: Constructing dichotomous keys  [width opportunity]

This builds on kingdom-level features (waypoint 2), binary decision logic (waypoint 3) and the purpose of classification (waypoint 1). After it, students can build a key that correctly identifies a set of organisms and has been tested by a peer. Waypoint 5 reuses this thinking: a classification tool is a made thing that can be revised.

:::keep-together

### Lesson 4.1: Building a first key
**Focus:** Students choose discriminating features and build a key for six organisms, aiming for each decision point to split the remaining group cleanly into two.

**Shape:** demonstrate → build → draft → check

**Cliff notes:**
- **Likely trouble spot:** Students pick features that don't separate the group (all six have legs), use a three-way split (colour: red, green or brown), or write questions that need the organism's name ("Is it a spider?").
- **What to say:** Ask "Can anyone check this by looking, without knowing the name?" and "Does this split the group roughly in half?" Use a feature table of the six organisms to spot the splits first.
- **If it isn't landing:** Provide the feature table with two features pre-filled and ask students to find the split before drawing the key.
- **Watch-out:** Peer testing belongs in 4.2. In this lesson students should test their own key against each organism but won't yet have it checked by someone else.

{{columns 22% 26% 26% 26%}}
| Minimum width | Consolidating | Steady | Advancing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Constructs a key for 6 organisms using observable features | Build from a partly completed key skeleton and feature table | Build the key from the feature table | Build a key with groups and subgroups for the set |
| Uses binary observable features at each decision point | Check each decision point with a "two answers, checkable by looking" prompt | Self-check each decision point against the two-answer test | Design a key for a novel set (everyday objects or invented creatures) and say what makes a feature useful |

:::

:::keep-together

### Lesson 4.2: Test it, break it, fix it
**Focus:** Students swap keys with a partner who tests it blind, record one revision after the test, and extend the key to 10 organisms.

**Shape:** investigate → evaluate → draft → reflect

**Cliff notes:**
- **Likely trouble spot:** When the peer reaches a wrong answer, students defend the key ("you used it wrong") instead of seeing which decision point failed.
- **What to say:** Treat the tester's result as data: "Which question sent them the wrong way, and why?" Ask the tester to say aloud what they were checking at each step.
- **If it isn't landing:** Give the tester a recording sheet that logs each decision and answer, so the failing point is visible on paper.
- **Watch-out:** The tester must not be told the organism names in advance, or the test proves nothing.

{{columns 22% 26% 26% 26%}}
| Minimum width | Consolidating | Steady | Advancing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Key correctly classifies all organisms in the set | Fix one flagged decision point with a teacher prompt | Revise after the peer test so all organisms are identified | Evaluate how well the key works and where it could still fail |
| Peer testing confirms the key gives correct identifications | Peer uses a recording sheet to log each step | Peer tests blind and reports the result | Compare the structure of a key built on First Nations criteria with a Linnaean key |

## Waypoint 5: Classification systems change over time  [width opportunity]

This builds on all earlier waypoints: students have now seen that classification is a purposeful, constructed tool and that keys can be tested and fixed. After it, students can give a real example of reclassification, say what evidence caused the change, and say what it shows about how science works. It is the end of the sequence.

:::keep-together

### Lesson 5.1: When science changes its mind about a group
**Focus:** Students work through one real reclassification, such as fungi being moved out of the plant kingdom, naming the old grouping, the new evidence and what the change tells us about science.

**Shape:** orient → teach → build → reflect → check

**Cliff notes:**
- **Likely trouble spot:** Students describe what moved without saying what evidence caused it, or conclude that reclassification means "scientists got it wrong" without weighing that against science correcting itself.
- **What to say:** Use the chain "old grouping → new evidence → what changed → what this shows about science" and ask students to fill each link in their own words.
- **If it isn't landing:** Provide a one-paragraph case card for a single reclassification and have students underline the evidence.
- **Watch-out:** Keep the evidence at a level students can follow (such as features seen under a microscope), and don't introduce DNA analysis as new content beyond the checkpoint. [teacher: choose and check your example]

| Explains what new evidence caused the change | Complete the "old → evidence → new" chain with sentence starters | Trace the evidence before and after for one organism | Evaluate whether reclassification shows science is unreliable or that it works, with an evidence-based argument |
