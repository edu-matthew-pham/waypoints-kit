<!-- _class: vocab -->
<!-- _header: '**Concept** What is a dichotomous key?' -->

:::: grid-2x1

> **Dichotomous key:** A tool for identifying an organism by answering a series of questions about its features. Each question has exactly two choices.
>
> **Decision point:** A place in the key where you choose between two options. Each one checks one feature.
>
> **Binary decision:** A choice between exactly two options. Every organism fits one option and only one.

> **KEY VOCABULARY**
>
> **dichotomous** — cut in two
>
> **decision point** — where the key asks you to choose
>
> **binary decision** — two options, only one can be true
>
> **observable feature** — something you can see or measure

::::

<!-- Speaker note: [MINIMUM] Dichotomous comes from the Greek for 'cut in two'. Insist on the phrase 'only one can be true': that is the binary logic students need for waypoint 4. -->

<!-- _header: '**Concept** Reading a key: Key A' -->

Key A identifies six garden animals. At each step, choose (a) or (b).

| Step | (a) If the organism… | (b) If the organism… |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | has a coiled shell on its back → **Garden snail** | has no coiled shell → go to 2 |
| **2** | has legs → go to 3 | has no legs → go to 5 |
| **3** | has 8 legs → **Spider** | does not have 8 legs → go to 4 |
| **4** | has 6 legs → **Ant** | has more than 6 legs → **Slater** |
| **5** | has a body made of many rings → **Earthworm** | has a smooth body with no rings → **Slug** |

<!-- Speaker note: [MINIMUM] Walk through the layout before using it: each numbered step has two options, and the arrow tells you where to go next. Students often skip the reading and follow arrows by habit, which is the failure this waypoint is built to prevent. [teacher: if you have your own key and organisms, use them and keep the rest] -->

<!-- _header: '**I Do: Worked Ex. 1** Using Key A: the slater' -->

Organism in my hand: a slater, with 14 legs, a grey plated back and no coiled shell.

| Step | Feature I am checking | The two options | My choice |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Coiled shell | has one / has none | none, so go to 2 |
| 2 | Legs | has legs / has no legs | has legs, so go to 3 |
| 3 | Eight legs | 8 / not 8 | not 8, so go to 4 |
| 4 | Six legs | 6 / more than 6 | more than 6, so **slater** |

> At this point I am checking **[feature]**. The two options are **[A]** and **[B]**.

<!-- Speaker note: [MINIMUM] Say the frame aloud at every step: 'At this point I am checking the feature. The two options are A and B.' This is the structured-reasoning move the node file asks for at this hinge. Students will copy it in We Do. -->

**At a decision point in a dichotomous key, what must be true of the two options?**

<!-- _class: cfu callout-ok -->
<!-- _header: '**Check** ✓ Answer' -->

**At a decision point in a dichotomous key, what must be true of the two options?**

- **A** An organism can fit both options
- **B** **There are exactly two options, and each organism fits only one**
- **C** There can be three options if needed
- **D** Each option checks a different feature

> **✓ Answer: B** — a binary decision has exactly two options, and each organism fits only one. A and C break the binary rule, and in D both options check the same feature.

<!-- Speaker note: [MINIMUM] This is the hinge concept in one question. If more than a few students miss it, redo Worked Ex. 1 with a different organism. -->

**"If a key leads you to an organism's name, you can be sure every decision you made was correct."**

> **✗ FALSE**
>
> **Why?** A wrong choice early on can still lead to a name: a wrong one. That is why you check the feature at every decision point.
